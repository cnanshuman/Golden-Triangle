DELHI — The Crown of Victoria

Delhi, the heart of India, has witnessed the rise and fall of empires
and became a symbol of freedom with India’s independence on
15 August 1947.

Old Delhi — The Mughal Legacy

The Mughals ruled India from 1526 to 1858, and Delhi was their imperial heart. During this golden era, they built some of the finest architectural wonders the world has ever seen — testaments to grandeur, art, and power.

Red Fort, built by Emperor Shah Jahan in the 17th century, stands as a symbol of Mughal architecture and India's independence to this day — it's from its ramparts that the Indian Prime Minister addresses the nation every Independence Day.

Facing it across the vibrant chaos of Chandni Chowk is Jama Masjid, one of the largest mosques in India, completed in 1656. Between these two monuments lies a maze of narrow lanes — bursting with the aroma of kebabs, the sound of prayer calls, the glitter of silver jewelry, and the shouts of traders who keep Old Delhi alive.

And at the very soul of this sensory world lies Khari Baoli — Asia's largest wholesale spice market, tucked just behind Fatehpuri Masjid at the western end of Chandni Chowk. For centuries, it has been the aromatic backbone of Delhi's cuisine and commerce. Walk through its crowded alleys and the air itself changes — thick with the warmth of dried chilies, the earthiness of cumin, the sweetness of cinnamon, and the sharp bite of black pepper. Sacks overflowing with turmeric, cardamom, star anise, and exotic dried herbs line every corner, painted in colors that feel almost unreal. It was this very trade in spices — precious as gold in the medieval world — that first drew foreign powers to India's shores. Standing in Khari Baoli, you don't just smell spices; you breathe in centuries of history.

New Delhi — The British Crown

When the British arrived under the banner of the East India Company, they came as traders. But commerce soon turned into conquest. By 1757, after the Battle of Plassey, Britain began to rule India — a faraway empire built on trade, resource extraction, and political manipulation. It is no coincidence that spices — black pepper, indigo, and opium — were among the most coveted prizes that drew these distant powers ever deeper into India's interior.

Under the British Raj, the capital was shifted from Calcutta to Delhi in 1911, marking the rise of New Delhi — a planned city of imperial ambition. Designed primarily by Sir Edwin Lutyens and Sir Herbert Baker, New Delhi became the embodiment of British power in the East.

The heart of New Delhi is Rashtrapati Bhavan, once known as the Viceroy's House — the seat of the British Governor-General, now the residence of the President of India. It's a fusion of Western classical lines and Indian motifs, symbolizing both dominance and dialogue. The wide avenues, sprawling gardens, and symmetry of the Lutyens' Zone reflect an attempt to impose order on the vibrant chaos of Old Delhi.

But beyond the architecture lay stories of exploitation — trade in opium, black pepper, indigo, and treasures like the Kohinoor Diamond that crossed oceans to adorn another crown. What began as trade ended as dominance, and through rebellion and resistance — most notably the Revolt of 1857 that first erupted in Delhi — the people's voice eventually reclaimed the city and the nation.

India Gate — The Memorial of Valor

At the center of New Delhi stands one of its most iconic landmarks — India Gate, a grand war memorial dedicated to nearly 84,000 Indian soldiers who lost their lives fighting for the British Indian Army during World War I, the Anglo-Afghan Wars, and other conflicts. Designed by the renowned architect Sir Edwin Lutyens in the 1920s, the monument reflects classical European architecture and draws inspiration from ancient Roman triumphal arches such as the Arch of Constantine in Rome. Rising proudly along the ceremonial Rajpath, India Gate has become far more than a memorial; it stands today as a symbol of sacrifice, honor, and national pride.

Carved into its massive sandstone walls are the names of thousands of soldiers, preserving the memory of those who never returned home. Beneath the arch burns the Amar Jawan Jyoti (“Flame of the Immortal Soldier”), which was added after the 1971 Indo-Pak War as a tribute to the unknown soldiers who gave their lives for independent India. The eternal flame became a powerful reminder of courage and devotion to the nation, watched over by soldiers standing guard day and night.

As the sun sets, the atmosphere around India Gate transforms completely. Families, tourists, street vendors, and children gather across the wide green lawns that surround the monument. The sound of laughter, the glow of lights, and the cool evening breeze give the area a unique energy that blends history with everyday life. Ice cream carts, food stalls, and evening walks turn the memorial grounds into one of Delhi’s most beloved public spaces, where remembrance and celebration exist side by side.

New Delhi — The British Crown

Delhi is not just a city; it is a living chronicle of civilization, shaped by centuries of empires, cultures, trade, and resistance. Kings, poets, traders, saints, and revolutionaries have all walked these same streets, leaving behind stories carved into stone and memory. From the peaceful spiritual gatherings at Nizamuddin Dargah to the colonial-era circles of Connaught Place, every corner of Delhi carries echoes of another age. Its forts, tombs, mosques, temples, and markets together tell the story of a city that has risen again and again through conquest, rebellion, and transformation.

In the crowded lanes of Khari Baoli, the air is still filled with the fragrance of spices that once connected India to the great trade routes of the world. The scent of cardamom, black pepper, cinnamon, and dried chilies serves as a reminder of the wealth and commerce that drew merchants and foreign powers toward Delhi for centuries. Walking through these historic markets feels like stepping into a world where the past still breathes through every alley and every voice.

Today, Old Delhi and New Delhi exist side by side as two worlds within one city — one preserving the vibrant chaos and soul of history, the other reflecting the vision of modern India. From the Mughal domes of Jama Masjid and the mighty walls of Red Fort to the grand colonial avenues leading to India Gate and Rashtrapati Bhavan, Delhi continues to blend history with modern life. It remains what it has always been — the beating heart, the living crown, and the timeless storyteller of India.

Taj Mahal — A Symbol of
Love and Sorrow

In the 17th century, a man lost the woman he loved most in the world. She was Mumtaz Mahal — his queen, his companion, his heart — who died in 1631 while giving birth to their fourteenth child. The man was Emperor Shah Jahan, and his grief was so vast, so consuming, that no words could contain it. So he did what only an emperor could do — he turned his grief into marble.

What followed was one of the greatest acts of devotion the world has ever seen. Over 20,000 artisans, craftsmen, and laborers worked for more than 20 years to bring his vision to life on the banks of the Yamuna River in Agra. The finest white marble was sourced from Makrana in Rajasthan. Precious stones — lapis lazuli, turquoise, jade, coral, and carnelian — were inlaid into its walls in intricate floral patterns, a technique known as pietra dura. Calligraphy from the Quran adorned its arched gateways. Four minarets rose at its corners, perfectly symmetrical, as if even the sky had been arranged in mourning. The result was the Taj Mahal — a white marvel rising from the earth like a prayer, pure and silent, its reflection shimmering in the long pool that stretches before it. Poets called it a teardrop on the face of eternity. Others called it the finest building ever raised by human hands. Both are right.

But the story does not end in beauty.
It ends in heartbreak.

Shah Jahan's own son, Aurangzeb, overthrew him in 1658 and imprisoned his father within the walls of Agra Fort — just a mile from the Taj Mahal. The man who had built the most magnificent tomb in the world was now a prisoner, stripped of his throne, his freedom, and his dignity. Yet even captivity could not take everything from him.

From a small window in the Musamman Burj tower of Agra Fort, Shah Jahan could see the Taj Mahal in the distance — its white dome gleaming in the morning light, fading to gold at dusk, and dissolving into silver under the moon. Every day, for the remaining eight years of his life, he sat by that window and gazed at the monument he had built for the woman he had lost. It is said that Aurangzeb, despite his cruelty toward his father, wrote him letters regularly — keeping him informed about the upkeep and condition of the Taj Mahal, knowing that it was the only comfort left to the old emperor.

When Shah Jahan died in 1666, he was laid to rest beside Mumtaz Mahal inside the Taj Mahal — the only asymmetrical element in an otherwise perfectly symmetrical structure, as if even in death, love refused to follow any rule.

The Taj Mahal is often called a symbol of love. And it is. But it is also a symbol of loss, of obsession, and of the strange and painful lengths to which the human heart will go when it cannot let go. It is romantic, yes — but it is also the story of a man who gave everything for love, and in the end, could only watch it from a window.

Rajasthan — The Land of the Maharajas

A land that belongs to warriors and color. A land where the desert blooms in festivals, where every fort tells a battle cry frozen in stone, and where the warmth of the people rivals the warmth of the sun itself. This is Rajasthan — the Pink City and beyond, a kingdom that never truly ended, only changed its crown.

But before we begin,
let me share an experience.

The first time I encountered a man from Rajasthan, I was not prepared for what walked through the door. He greeted me with a wide, unhurried smile and the words — "Khama Ghani." A traditional Rajasthani greeting, it means I bow to you with humility — and in that single phrase, you understand everything about the culture that produced it. Grace. Dignity. Respect without transaction.

The man himself was a definition made flesh. Well-built, somewhere in his sixties, with a magnificent mustache that curled at the ends with the confidence of a man who had never doubted himself a single day in his life. On his head sat a grand Rajasthani pagri — a turban wrapped in the colors of royalty. His clothes were traditional, his posture straight as a minaret. He was a chauffeur by profession. A gentleman by nature. Mr. Singh. And he was always smiling — not the polite, performative smile of someone trying to please, but the deep, settled smile of someone who is simply at peace with the world he inhabits.

That smile, I later understood, is Rajasthan itself.

Jaipur — The Pink City

Jaipur, the capital of Rajasthan, is called the Pink City for a reason rooted not in romance, but in royal hospitality. In 1876, Maharaja Sawai Ram Singh ordered the entire city to be painted terracotta pink — a color traditionally associated with warmth and welcome — in honor of the visit of Prince Albert of Wales. The color stuck, and today, the old walled city glows in shades of rose and rust, as if the city itself is perpetually dressed for a celebration.

Walking through its bazaars is an assault on the senses in the most magnificent way — block-printed fabrics spilling from shop fronts, the smell of spices mingling with incense, the clatter of bangles, and the calls of vendors selling everything from saffron to semi-precious stones. Jaipur does not whisper. It announces itself.

Hawa Mahal — The Palace of Winds

Standing at the edge of the old city like a crown placed gently on the skyline is the Hawa Mahal — the Palace of Winds. Built in 1799 by Maharaja Sawai Pratap Singh, it is one of the most instantly recognizable facades in all of India — a five-storey honeycomb of 953 small windows, each latticed with intricately carved pink sandstone screens called jharokhas.

It was built with a specific and remarkable purpose. The women of the royal household, bound by the tradition of purdah, were not permitted to appear in public. Yet life — festivals, processions, the bustle of the marketplace below — continued without them. Hawa Mahal was their answer. From behind those carved screens, the royal women could watch the world pass by unseen, while the wind — channeled through the lattice — kept the interior cool even in the fierce Rajasthani summer. A palace built not for power, but for the quiet, dignified desire to simply see.

From the outside, especially at sunrise when the pink sandstone catches the first light of the morning, Hawa Mahal looks less like a building and more like a wave of stone that forgot to fall.

Amber Fort — The Fortress of Gold

Eleven kilometers from the heart of Jaipur, perched on a ridge of the Aravalli Hills and gazing down at the still waters of Maota Lake below, stands Amber Fort — one of the most breathtaking fortresses in the world.

Built in 1592 by Raja Man Singh I and expanded over generations by successive Maharajas, Amber Fort is a testament to the synthesis of Rajput and Mughal architecture. Its construction used pale yellow and pink sandstone, and its walls rise in layers up the hillside — battlements and bastions climbing toward the sky as if the mountain itself decided to become a palace.

The entrance through the Suraj Pol — the Sun Gate — opens into a vast courtyard where the world seems to expand. Beyond it lies the Sheesh Mahal — the Palace of Mirrors — perhaps the most dazzling room in all of Rajasthan. Its walls and ceiling are encrusted entirely with tiny convex mirror pieces set into plasterwork of extraordinary delicacy. Light a single candle inside, and the room erupts into a thousand stars. It is said that a Maharaja could see the reflection of the entire night sky without ever stepping outside.

The fort is also home to the Diwan-i-Aam (Hall of Public Audience) and the Diwan-i-Khas (Hall of Private Audience) — spaces where justice was dispensed and diplomacy was conducted, where the Maharajas received kings and emperors on equal terms. Walking through Amber Fort is not like visiting a museum. It is like stepping into a conversation between centuries.

Nahargarh Fort — The Tiger Fort

Watching over Jaipur from the ridge of the Aravalli Hills like a silent, eternal guardian is Nahargarh Fort — popularly known as the Tiger Fort. Built in 1734 by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II, it was originally constructed as a retreat and a defensive stronghold to protect the city below. Its name, Nahargarh, means "abode of tigers" — a reflection of both the wild terrain that surrounds it and the fierce spirit of the Rajput warriors who built it.

Unlike the grandeur of Amber Fort, Nahargarh carries a quieter, more rugged beauty. Its thick walls snake along the uneven ridge of the hills, and the views from its ramparts are among the finest in Rajasthan — the entire Pink City stretching out below, fading into the golden haze of the desert horizon. At sunset, when the city lights begin to flicker on and the sky turns from orange to violet, the view from Nahargarh is something that stays with you long after you have left.

Inside, the Madhavendra Bhawan — a palace built for the Maharaja's nine queens — is a fascinating maze of interconnected suites, each identical in design so that no queen could claim more favor than another. Even in architecture, Rajasthan understood the politics of love.

Rajasthan — Then and Now

Rajasthan is not a destination. It is an experience that begins the moment someone greets you with Khama Ghani and means it. It is Mr. Singh's curling mustache and unhurried smile. It is the wind moving through 953 stone windows. It is a candle igniting a thousand mirror-stars. It is a fort that watches over a city like a father watching over his children.

The warriors are still here — in the pride of the people, in the stories etched into every stone, in the colors that refuse to fade. Rajasthan does not simply preserve its history. It lives inside it, warmly and without apology, every single day.
